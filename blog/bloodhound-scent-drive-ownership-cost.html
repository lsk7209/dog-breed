Bloodhound Reality: Scent Drive, Fencing, Ears, and Drool

A Bloodhound follows its nose, wanders, and bays loudly. Plan for secure fencing, ear and wrinkle care, drool, and recall limits with this owner worksheet.

Published: 2026-10-05Sources checked: 2Educational planning guide

A Bloodhound is a gentle, famously nosy scent hound whose drive to follow a trail, tendency to wander, and loud voice mean secure containment and patient handling matter more than almost anything else you buy.

The nose leads everything

According to VCA, the Bloodhound stands about 23 to 27 inches, weighs roughly 80 to 110 pounds, and lives about 7 to 10 years. It is one of the oldest scent-tracking breeds, likely descended from the St. Hubert's hound, and VCA notes it still holds tracking records for the length and staleness of trails it can follow. VCA describes it as a placid, amiable, and reliable housedog that is generally friendly toward strangers and good with other dogs and pets.

VCA is direct about what that heritage means at home: the Bloodhound likes to follow its nose, tends to wander, and does not reliably come when called. It also often barks and bays loudly when excited or on a trail, and it tends to be stubborn and methodical, doing best with reward-based training built around food. Plan for a dog whose instincts will sometimes outvote your recall, and whose easygoing charm comes packaged with a powerful, independent drive.

Fencing, leashes, and recall limits

Because a Bloodhound will lock onto a scent, VCA warns that care must be taken off leash since the dog gets caught up following trails and is not overly obedient or inclined to come when called. Secure fencing and reliable leash habits are the backbone of safe ownership, not optional extras. Our fence escape risk review and scent hound recall planning guide walk through how to think about containment for a determined tracker.

VCA does note Bloodhounds tend to do well at dog parks, and that their physical exercise needs can be met with a long walk or short run where they prefer to walk, sniff, and explore rather than sprint. The ASPCA reinforces the general rule to keep a dog leashed outdoors unless it is in a secured, fenced area. The goal for this breed is to give the nose plenty of legitimate work inside boundaries you have actually verified, so that following a scent never turns into following it out of your yard.

Ears, wrinkles, and drool

VCA describes the coat as short, thick, and tight, needing only weekly brushing, with average shedding. But it adds two upkeep points that define the breed: the mouth and wrinkles should be examined, cleaned, and dried regularly, and owners should be prepared for drool. For a dog that eats and drinks enthusiastically, that cleanup is a daily habit rather than an occasional chore.

The long, low-hanging ears and facial folds are worth a routine check. This guide gives no medical advice and diagnoses nothing, but our ear-care routine planning guide helps you build a habit and know what to raise with a veterinarian. VCA also notes Bloodhounds tend to become overweight, so pair coat and ear checks with a body-condition check; keeping the dog lean supports its joints and its overall comfort as it ages, and VCA lists a lifespan of about 7 to 10 years.

Feeding and getting set up

VCA notes Bloodhound puppies should be fed a large-breed growth food, which slows the growing rate but not the final size and may reduce the incidence or severity of hip dysplasia in adults, and that adults should eat a balanced diet with calories restricted if weight climbs. The ASPCA's general dog care guidance adds that larger dogs or those prone to bloat may do better on two smaller meals, a routine worth discussing with your veterinarian.

Before bringing a Bloodhound home, work through the ASPCA supply checklist: quality food, dishes, safe toys, brush and comb, a collar with license and ID tag, a sturdy leash, a crate sized for a large dog, and a warm bed. For this breed, add a secure long-line for sniff walks and cloths for ear and drool care, and follow the ASPCA's advice to pair an ID tag with a microchip, which matters more than usual for a dog inclined to wander.

A week that tests the fit

Picture a Saturday. You walk for a long, sniffy hour on a secure leash, letting the nose do the work. At home the yard fence is checked for gaps because a determined tracker will find one. After meals you wipe drool and check the ears and folds. When a neighbor's grill fires up, your Bloodhound announces it with a deep bay.

  • Daily: a long leashed walk focused on sniffing; ear and drool check.
  • Weekly: brush the short coat; inspect fencing for weak points.
  • Ongoing: practice recall while accepting it may never be fully reliable outdoors.

If a strong-willed, vocal tracker sounds appealing, a Bloodhound rewards patience; if you need off-leash reliability, read our recall training risk guide first.

Questions to ask a breeder, rescue, or vet

  • Ask a breeder or rescue how strong this dog's wandering and trailing drive is.
  • Ask how the dog behaves on leash and whether it has ever slipped a fence or door.
  • Ask about the dog's ear and skin-fold history.
  • Ask a veterinarian how to keep the ears and folds clean and what warning signs to watch.
  • Ask about weight management, since VCA notes Bloodhounds tend to become overweight.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Trusting off-leash freedom when VCA says the breed follows scent and doesn't reliably recall.
  • Assuming a fence is secure without checking for gaps a tracker will exploit.
  • Neglecting ear and wrinkle care until a problem develops.
  • Being caught off guard by the loud baying in a close-neighbor setting.
  • Letting weight climb, which VCA flags as a tendency in the breed.

What this guide cannot tell you

This guide cannot predict an individual Bloodhound's drive, health, or your local costs, and it offers no diagnosis, treatment, or dosing. Temperament and health vary between dogs. Use local quotes for the worksheet, verify any containment plan with your own property, and consult a licensed veterinarian for ear, skin, weight, or any other health question about your specific dog.

FAQ

Can a Bloodhound be trusted off leash?
Generally not in unfenced areas. VCA states the Bloodhound tends to get caught up following scent trails and is not overly obedient or inclined to come when called, so off-leash freedom carries real risk. Most owners rely on secure fencing and leashed walks, and treat recall as a skill worth practicing but never fully depending on outdoors.
How much grooming does a Bloodhound need?
The coat is low-effort: VCA describes it as short, thick, and tight, needing only weekly brushing, with average shedding. The bigger upkeep is elsewhere. VCA advises examining, cleaning, and drying the mouth and wrinkles regularly, and warns owners to be prepared for drool. Budget time and supplies for ear and skin-fold care rather than for coat maintenance.
Is a Bloodhound loud?
It can be. VCA notes Bloodhounds often bark and bay loudly when excited or on a trail, and rates the breed's barking high. The deep, carrying voice is part of the scent-hound heritage. In apartments or homes with close neighbors, plan for this tendency and consider training and enrichment to manage it, rather than expecting a quiet dog.
