Dog Licenses, Microchips, and Registration Costs Owners Forget

Dog licenses, microchips, and chip registration are small recurring costs new owners overlook. Learn what each does and how to budget for all three.

Published: 2026-10-06Sources checked: 2Educational planning guide

A microchip is a permanent ID injected under the skin, a chip registration keeps your contact details attached to that ID, and a dog license is a local government requirement in many areas. Each is a small cost, but together they are easy to forget until you need them.

Three different things that all identify your dog

New owners often lump these together, but they do separate jobs. According to the American Kennel Club, a microchip is a radio-frequency identification transponder about the size of a grain of rice, injected under the loose skin between the shoulder blades, with no battery and no moving parts. When a vet or shelter scans it, the chip transmits a unique ID number. The AKC describes the implant as no more invasive than a vaccination and something that can be done in your vet's office.

  • Microchip: the permanent physical ID under the skin.
  • Chip registration: the database entry that ties that ID number to your name and phone.
  • Dog license: a local government registration, separate from the chip, that many cities and counties require.

The AKC is clear that the chip alone does nothing useful unless you register the ID number with a national pet recovery database and keep your contact information current. It also notes that different registries offer different services, and that some are part of a shared lookup network so a scanning shelter can check across many registries. The takeaway for a new owner: implanting the chip is only step one, registration is step two, and your local license is a separate obligation altogether.

A microchip is not a tracker

One of the most common misunderstandings is that a microchip can locate a lost dog. The AKC is explicit that microchips are not GPS devices: a chip cannot tell you where your dog is. Instead, it lets almost any veterinarian or shelter that scans your dog pull up your contact information and reach you. The AKC also recommends your dog keep wearing a collar and tags, because a chip and a visible tag work together rather than replacing each other.

In its travel guide, the AKC echoes this layered approach: it suggests a sturdy collar with identification tags showing the dog's name and your phone number, plus a permanent form of identification such as a microchip, and keeping a recent photo and copy of records on hand. Enter both landline and cell numbers for everyone responsible for the dog so a found-dog call does not go unanswered, and keep those details updated with the registry over time. If you move or change your number, updating the registry should be on the same checklist as changing your address everywhere else.

Why these small costs matter

It is tempting to treat identification as an afterthought, but the AKC frames it as a core safety measure. It cites a statistic that one in three pets becomes lost at some point in their lives, which is why it recommends a layered approach: a permanent microchip that a shelter can scan plus a visible collar tag anyone can read. The two are complementary, not redundant.

The AKC also explains why registration is the part people forget and the part that matters most in an emergency. A found dog gets scanned, the chip returns an ID number, and whoever scans it looks that number up in a registry. If the registry has your current phone number, you get a call; if it points to an old number or was never completed, the trail goes cold. Some registries, the AKC notes, add services such as a lost-pet alert that broadcasts your dog's information to local vets, shelters, and volunteers. Keeping your details current is the single highest-value habit in this whole topic.

A first-month checklist for a new dog

Here is a realistic sequence for a dog you just brought home, so nothing slips through the cracks:

  1. Confirm whether your dog already has a chip; ask the vet to scan at the first visit.
  2. If chipped, look up the number and transfer registration into your name.
  3. If not chipped, discuss implanting one at a routine appointment.
  4. Register the chip ID with a recovery database and enter every household contact number.
  5. Check your city or county rules and apply for a dog license if required.
  6. Put a collar tag on with a current phone number as a visible backup.

The step people most often skip is the second one. A rescued or rehomed dog may already carry a chip that still points to a shelter, a breeder, or a previous owner. If you never update it, a future scan sends the finder to someone who no longer has your dog. When you adopt, ask the shelter or rescue what registry the chip is in and how to transfer it. Questions to ask your vet and local office

  • To your vet: Does my dog already have a chip, and what number and registry is it linked to?
  • To your vet: Is the implant a one-time cost, or is there a separate registration step I handle myself?
  • To the registry: Is registration a one-time fee or a renewal, and how do I update my contact details?
  • To your city or county: Is a dog license required here, how often does it renew, and does proof of rabies vaccination factor in?
  • To your vet: Can you scan the chip at each visit to confirm it still reads correctly?

Common identification mistakes

  • Chipping but never registering. The AKC notes an unregistered chip will not help reunite you with your dog.
  • Letting contact info go stale. A chip pointing to an old phone number is a dead end.
  • Treating a chip as a tracker. It gives no location; a visible tag still matters.
  • Forgetting the local license. Licensing is a separate requirement from chipping in many areas.
  • Not confirming a rescue or rehomed dog's chip. The chip may still list a previous owner.
  • Assuming one registry talks to another automatically. Register in a database and keep that specific record current.

What this guide cannot tell you

This guide cannot tell you the exact price of a microchip, registration, or license in your area, because those depend on your clinic, your chosen registry, and your local government. It cannot interpret your city's licensing ordinance or tell you which registry to choose. It also does not cover medical questions about the implant procedure itself. Ask your veterinarian about the procedure, confirm registry terms directly with the provider, and check your local government office for licensing rules that apply where you live.

FAQ

Does a microchip track my dog's location?
No. The American Kennel Club is clear that a microchip is not a GPS device and cannot tell you where your dog is. It stores a unique ID number that a vet or shelter can scan to look up your contact information. That is why the AKC recommends keeping your details current in the registry and having your dog wear a collar tag as a visible backup.
Is registering the chip separate from implanting it?
Often yes. According to the AKC, the chip does no good unless you register the ID number with a recovery database and enter your contact details. Implanting the chip and registering it can be separate steps, sometimes with separate costs. Ask your vet what is included, then confirm registration and renewal terms directly with the database provider.
Do I still need a dog license if my dog is chipped?
Usually yes, because a dog license is a local government requirement that is separate from microchipping. A chip identifies your dog; a license is a civic registration many cities and counties mandate, sometimes tied to proof of rabies vaccination. Check with your city or county office for the rules, renewal frequency, and any documentation they require where you live.
