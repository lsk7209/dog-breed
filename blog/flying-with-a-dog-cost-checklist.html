Flying With a Dog: Cabin, Cargo, and Health Paperwork Checklist

Planning to fly with your dog? Learn how cabin and cargo travel differ, what health paperwork you may need, and how to budget the full trip cost.

Published: 2026-10-09Sources checked: 2Educational planning guide

Small dogs often fly in the cabin inside a carrier that fits under the seat, while larger dogs usually travel in cargo, and both require you to confirm the airline's rules and your dog's health paperwork before you book.

How dogs actually travel on a plane

According to the American Kennel Club, how your dog flies depends mostly on size. Small dogs can often travel in the cabin as long as they stay inside an approved carrier that fits beneath the seat in front of you, and they must remain in the carrier for the flight rather than sitting on a lap. Larger dogs generally travel in cargo in an airline-approved crate.

The AKC notes that airlines usually limit the number of dogs per flight and require advance reservations, and that you cannot buy your dog its own seat. Its travel guide adds that spots are accepted on a first-come, first-served basis, so booking early matters. For cargo travel, the AKC recommends direct flights to reduce handling and warns that airlines may suspend cargo pet travel during extreme heat or cold; it advises checking the temperature at both the departure and destination points. Since 2021, the AKC adds, emotional support animals are treated as pets and must follow standard pet policies, while trained service dogs assisting people with disabilities are the only dogs permitted to travel openly in the cabin.

Size limits and the carrier that fits

The AKC explains that weight limits vary by airline, which is why you should contact the carrier directly before booking. As a general guide it cites, in-cabin travel often requires the combined weight of dog and carrier to be under 20 pounds, though many U.S. airlines focus more on whether the carrier fits under the seat than on a strict weight cutoff. For cargo or checked baggage, the AKC states the combined dog-and-crate limit usually maxes out between 70 and 150 pounds.

The AKC advises that carriers be waterproof and large enough for your dog to sit, stand, lie down, and turn around comfortably, while still fitting beneath the seat in front of you. For cargo crates, the AKC's travel guide lists features to look for: strong construction with handles and grips and no interior protrusions, a leak-proof bottom with absorbent material, ventilation on opposing sides with exterior rims so airflow is not blocked, and a "Live Animal" label with arrows showing the upright position plus your name, address, and phone number. Health paperwork and entry rules

The AKC advises scheduling a veterinary visit before flying to confirm your dog is healthy enough to travel, verify vaccinations, and get an updated rabies certificate if needed. In its travel guide, the AKC adds that you should take your dog's shot records with you because health certifications may be required for airline travel, and that each airline has its own regulations while each state has its own travel requirements. Documentation depends on where you are going, and international travel or dogs entering the United States can require more.

The AKC reports that under CDC regulations effective August 2024, dogs entering the United States must be at least 6 months old, have an ISO-compliant microchip, and be immunized against rabies, and that returning or importing owners must complete the CDC Dog Import Form before arrival. It adds that additional requirements may apply to dogs arriving from countries designated high risk for rabies. Confirm the current rules with your airline and destination well before your trip.

A realistic travel day

Here is how a smooth in-cabin travel day tends to go, drawing on the AKC's guidance:

  1. Confirm your dog's reservation at the ticket counter before security.
  2. At the security checkpoint, take your dog out of the carrier so the carrier can be screened, then reload.
  3. Use a designated pet relief area after security so your dog can go before boarding.
  4. Keep your dog in the carrier under the seat for the flight; a familiar chew or scented item can help.
  5. Give yourself extra time and keep all documents easy to reach.

The AKC suggests preparing your dog well before the trip by introducing the carrier early and building a positive association with praise and treats, and by exposing your dog to busy, dog-friendly environments so crowds and noise feel less alarming. If your dog is prone to motion sickness, tell your veterinarian in advance. And be aware of what to pack: the AKC's checklist includes vaccination and health records, portable bowls, a small supply of food in your carry-on, treats, poop bags, wipes, and a safe chew, with enough of any prescription food and medication for the whole trip plus possible delays. The AKC also cautions against giving any medication before travel without first consulting your veterinarian, since some sedatives can raise the risk of heart or breathing problems and are often not allowed by airlines.

Should your dog fly at all?

Not every dog is a good candidate for air travel, and the AKC is direct about this. It notes that airports, planes, crowds, unfamiliar sounds, and new environments can be a lot for a dog to handle, and it recommends talking with your veterinarian about whether flying is a good fit before you plan a trip. The AKC's travel guide adds a blunt reminder that some dogs simply do not enjoy traveling, and that staying home with a sitter may be the kinder choice.

Airlines also make clear that verifying a dog's health and ability to fly is the owner's responsibility, so this is a decision to make deliberately rather than by default. Consider your dog's age, health conditions, and temperament honestly. Questions to ask the airline and your vet

  • To the airline: Can my dog travel in the cabin on this aircraft and route, and what is the exact carrier size limit?
  • To the airline: How many pets are allowed per flight, and is a reservation required now?
  • To the airline: Are there breed, age, or seasonal cargo restrictions on my dates?
  • To your vet: Is my dog healthy enough to fly, and do you have concerns about the cabin or cargo environment?
  • To your vet: What documents will I need for my destination, and are vaccinations current?

Common flying-with-a-dog mistakes

  • Booking before calling the airline. The AKC stresses that rules and pet counts vary, so confirm first.
  • Buying a carrier that does not fit under the seat. Fit, not just weight, often decides cabin eligibility.
  • Leaving paperwork to the last minute. Certificates and import forms take time.
  • Sedating without veterinary guidance. The AKC warns this can be risky and airline-restricted.
  • Ignoring seasonal cargo suspensions. Extreme heat or cold can ground cargo pet travel.

What this guide cannot tell you

This guide cannot give you the exact pet fee for your airline, route, or dates, because those change and differ between carriers. It cannot confirm the current entry requirements for your destination, which are set by governments and can change. It is not medical advice about whether your individual dog should fly. Contact your airline directly for current fees and carrier rules, check official government sources such as the CDC for entry requirements, and ask your veterinarian about your dog's fitness to travel.

FAQ

Can my dog ride in the cabin with me?
The American Kennel Club says many airlines allow small dogs in the cabin if they stay inside an approved carrier that fits under the seat in front of you, and the dog must remain in the carrier during the flight. Weight and size limits vary by airline and aircraft, so contact the carrier directly before booking. Larger dogs generally must travel in cargo.
What paperwork do I need to fly with a dog?
It depends on your destination. The AKC recommends a veterinary visit to confirm health and current vaccinations, and possibly an updated rabies certificate. For dogs entering the United States, it reports CDC rules effective August 2024 require the dog be at least 6 months old, have an ISO-compliant microchip, be vaccinated against rabies, and that owners complete a CDC import form before arrival.
Should I give my dog a sedative before the flight?
Not without veterinary guidance. The AKC warns that some tranquilizers and sedatives can increase the risk of heart or respiratory problems in dogs and generally are not allowed by airlines. Never give any medication before travel without first consulting your veterinarian, who can advise whether your individual dog needs anything and what is safe for its health and the trip.
