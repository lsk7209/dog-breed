BreedWise Methodology

How BreedWise separates breed health-risk signals, cost planning assumptions, source quality, and editorial limits.

BreedWise uses a planning-first method: separate what a source supports from what a reader still needs to verify.

Evidence layers

We distinguish veterinary guidelines, university health resources, regulatory pages, breed-club health programs, OFA/CHIC references, peer-reviewed studies, owner anecdotes, and commercial cost reports. These sources answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Cost planning

Cost ranges are presented as planning context, not quotes. BreedWise separates first-year setup, annual routine care, lifetime ownership studies, breed-specific recurring costs, and emergency reserves. When national studies are cited, the article should explain that local prices and individual dogs vary.

Health boundaries

Articles can identify questions and watchlists, but they do not diagnose or recommend treatment. A statement such as "ask about hips" is acceptable planning guidance. A statement telling an owner how to treat lameness is not.

Updates and corrections

Pages should be corrected when source links break, facts change, or wording overstates certainty. Correction requests should include the URL, the sentence in question, and a source for review.

Advertising separation

Advertising or affiliate relationships, if added later, should not determine article conclusions, source interpretation, or breed recommendations.

What a source can and cannot answer

BreedWise uses sources by job, not by prestige alone. A public data series can describe the movement of a defined category, but it cannot quote a nearby provider or predict a household bill. A weather or air-quality observation can describe a recorded condition, but it cannot assess an individual dog's health or replace an official alert. A breed, veterinary, university, or health-program reference can explain a documented topic or record category, but it cannot diagnose a dog through a web page. Articles should state that boundary near the claim rather than borrowing certainty from the source name.

When a page combines more than one source, the reader should be able to see what each contribution does: public source for the shared definition or measured field, local record for the real-world constraint, and qualified professional input when a decision involves health, treatment, safety, or another individual assessment. Owner anecdotes may help identify questions, but they are not treated as medical proof or as a replacement for documented records.

Public-data snapshot rules

The Cost Data hub uses specified U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Price Index series through the BLS Public Data API v2. The Outdoor Risk hub uses National Weather Service forecast data and AirNow observations through their published services. Each relevant page records an update date or time and identifies its source type. A snapshot is retained as a transparent record of what the source returned when the page was built; it must not be presented as a live quote, a current emergency notice, or an individual recommendation.

For definitions and current source material, readers can check the BLS CPI program, the National Weather Service API documentation, and the AirNow API documentation. If the upstream data changes, availability is interrupted, or a page's wording claims more than the source supports, the page should be corrected rather than silently treated as current.

Review and correction path

Before a source-backed page is updated, the editor should identify the exact URL, the source field or statement being changed, the snapshot or access date, and whether the change affects a definition, a reported value, or the page's interpretation. Corrections do not require a visitor to agree with a conclusion; they require enough detail to check the underlying claim. Send that material through Contact. The site may clarify wording, repair a broken source link, add a limit, or remove a claim when verification does not support it.

How a reader can audit a page

A reader should be able to identify the page's subject, the public source or source class, the update or access context, the local fact still needed, and the editorial limit. If any one of those is missing, the useful response is not to fill the gap with confidence. It is to mark the point for verification, use the correction path, or ask the appropriate qualified source. This is particularly important when an article touches a cost commitment, an environmental condition, or a health-related question.

Reader decision boundary

BreedWise helps readers organize questions about ownership cost, routine, records, and environmental planning. It does not diagnose pets, prescribe treatment, rank insurers, guarantee affordability, or decide whether a particular dog is appropriate for a particular person. For decisions involving health, treatment, urgent conditions, local rules, or individual suitability, use the relevant official authority or qualified professional and keep the local documents that support the household plan.
